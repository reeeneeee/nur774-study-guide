## 1. Innocent murmurs
**What they are:** the sound of normal blood flowing through a normal heart. Nothing is broken. **Up to 80% of children** have one at some point.

**Innocent = the 7 S's (Box 38.2):**
- **Soft:** grade I–III, and localized (it doesn't spread)
- **Systolic:** except the venous hum, which is continuous
- **Short:** peaks in the first half of systole
- **Single:** no clicks, snaps, or extra sounds
- **Small area:** heard best at the LLSB or pulmonic area, and rarely radiates
- **Sweet:** musical, vibratory, sometimes blowing (never harsh)
- **Sensitive:** changes with position, and may disappear with Valsalva or gentle pressure on the neck vein

**Also expected with an innocent murmur:**
- Normal vital signs, a normal ECG, and a healthy child
- It may come and go between visits
- It gets **louder with fever, anemia, exercise, anxiety, or pain**, because these increase cardiac output

**Teaching for families:** the heart is normal. The murmur may come and go. **No activity limits and no special precautions.**

### The 4 innocent murmurs (Table 38.5)
| Murmur | Age | Where | Clues | Can be mistaken for |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Still** | 2–6 yrs | LLSB/apex, doesn't radiate | "Twangy string," vibratory, **louder lying down**, softer standing, grade II | Small VSD, IHSS |
| **Pulmonary flow murmur of childhood** | 8–14 yrs, thin-chested | Pulmonic area | Soft, blowing, **normal split S2**, no click or thrill, grade I–II | ASD, pulmonic stenosis |
| **Pulmonary flow murmur of infancy** (peripheral pulmonic stenosis) | Newborns, especially preemies | LUSB → spreads to **both axillae and the back** | Soft, **gone by 6 months** | Supravalvular PS or AS |
| **Venous hum** | 2–8 yrs | Upper chest under the clavicles, when upright | Continuous swishing that **disappears when lying down, turning the head, or pressing the neck vein** | **PDA** |

> 🧠 **"Still = Strings, Supine louder." "Hum goes Home when you lie down."**

### Red flags: possibly PATHOLOGIC (refer to cardiology)
- **Any diastolic murmur**
- A thrill, which means grade IV or louder
- A pansystolic (holosystolic) murmur
- A continuous murmur that **can't be made to go away**
- Clicks, opening snaps, or an S4
- Fixed split S2, or a loud (accentuated) S2
- A **harsh** murmur, or one that **doesn't change with position**
- A child with a syndrome linked to heart defects (for example, **trisomy 21**)

> 🧠 **"Diastolic is always Dangerous."** **"Loud ≠ bad, soft ≠ safe."** A small VSD can be loud, and a large defect may make no murmur at all.

## 2. Grading murmurs (Levine I–VI)
| Grade | What it sounds like |
|---|---|
| **I** | Barely audible. You hear it faintly only after listening carefully. |
| **II** | Soft, but easy to hear |
| **III** | Moderately loud, **no thrill** |
| **IV** | Loud, heard over a wide area, **palpable thrill** (the thrill starts here) |
| **V** | Heard with the stethoscope **barely touching** the chest, plus a thrill |
| **VI** | Heard **without** the stethoscope on the chest (rare) |

> 🧠 **"4 = you can FEEL it. 5 = barely touching. 6 = not touching."**

**Key facts:**
- **The grade does NOT tell you how severe the problem is.**
- The grade can change when the child moves from lying down to sitting.

**To fully describe a murmur, include:** timing (systolic, diastolic, or continuous), location, radiation, quality, duration, pitch, and grade.

**Timing:**
- **Systolic** (between S1 and S2): most murmurs, and every innocent murmur
- **Diastolic** (between S2 and S1): **always pathologic**
- **Continuous** (through both systole and diastole): most often a **PDA**. Tell it apart from a venous hum.

**Where to listen:**
- **URSB:** aortic stenosis click, venous hum
- **ULSB:** pulmonic stenosis click, ASD, PDA, pulmonary flow murmurs
- **LLSB:** VSD, Still, HCM, tricuspid regurgitation
- **Apex:** mitral regurgitation, the MVP click
- **Erb point:** aortic ejection click

## 3. Fetal circulation (study guide, Section 12)
**Before birth:**
- The **placenta** does the lungs' job, so blood mostly skips the lungs.
- The umbilical **vein** brings oxygenated blood **to** the baby.
- The umbilical **arteries** take blood **back** to the placenta.

**The 3 shunts:**
| Shunt | What it does | Mnemonic |
|---|---|---|
| **Ductus venosus** | Skips the liver | "Venosus skips the liVer" |
| **Foramen ovale** | Right atrium → left atrium | "Ovale = Open door between the atria" |
| **Ductus arteriosus** | Pulmonary artery → aorta (skips the lungs) | "Arteriosus connects the 2 Arteries" |

**At birth:**
1. The lungs expand, so **pulmonary resistance drops**.
2. The cord is clamped, so **systemic resistance rises**.
3. Left atrial pressure rises, which pushes the **foramen ovale** closed.
4. More oxygen and less prostaglandin make the **ductus arteriosus** constrict.

**From Chapter 38:** the ductus closes **functionally in 12–72 hours** and **permanently in 2–3 weeks** in term babies.

> 🧠 **"Prostaglandin keeps it Patent. Indomethacin Interrupts (closes) it."**

**Why this matters:** after birth, lung resistance keeps falling over the first **2–8 weeks**. More blood then crosses a VSD to the lungs, which is why a VSD murmur and heart failure appear **weeks after a normal newborn exam**.

## 4. ACYANOTIC defects (Fig 38.6)
### A. Left-to-right shunts: too much blood goes to the lungs
> 🧠 **"The D's flood the lungs."** ASD, VSD, PDA, and AVSD all end in D.

**ASD (hole between the atria)**
- Most common type: **secundum**, near the foramen ovale. **Primum** is lower and seen in **Down syndrome**.
- Often no symptoms. Some children are thin, tire easily, or get frequent colds or pneumonia.
- The murmur often isn't noticed until **2–3 years** old.
- **Wide, fixed split S2.** There is also a soft systolic murmur at the pulmonic area, caused by extra flow across the **pulmonary valve**, not by the hole itself.
- ECG: right axis deviation and an **rSR′ pattern in V1**.
- **Treatment:** small holes may close on their own. Device closure if the child weighs **more than 15 kg**, then **aspirin for 6 months** and endocarditis prophylaxis for 6 months. No exercise limits.
- **If untreated:** right-sided heart failure, clots crossing the hole to the body (paradoxical emboli), pulmonary hypertension.

> 🧠 **"ASD = Always Split, Doesn't change."**

**VSD (hole between the ventricles)**
- **The most common** heart defect. The most common type is **perimembranous**.
- **No murmur at birth.** As lung resistance falls (2–8 weeks), the loud murmur and heart failure appear.
- **Small VSD:** harsh, high-pitched holosystolic murmur at the **LLSB**; the child is otherwise normal. Most close by about **age 4**. **No endocarditis prophylaxis.**
- **Large VSD:** low-pitched holosystolic murmur, a diastolic rumble at the apex, a thrill, and heart failure with S3 or S4.
- A **higher-pitched murmur over time means the hole is getting smaller.**
- **Heart failure care:** digoxin, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, high-calorie formula (24/27/30 kcal/oz), and an NG tube if needed.
- Device closure if the child weighs **more than 6 kg**.

> 🧠 **"VSD = Very loud. Small hole, loud howl."**

**AV septal defect (AV canal)**
- **Complete** = primum ASD + **one shared AV valve** + inlet VSD.
- Strongly linked to **Down syndrome**. ECG: **superior axis**. Repaired **before 6 months** of age.
- **Watch out:** some babies with Down syndrome have a **loud single S2 and almost no murmur**. That means pulmonary hypertension, not a healthy heart.

**PDA (open ductus arteriosus)**
- Seen in **45–80% of preemies under 1750 g**. Girls are affected 3 times as often as boys.
- Right after birth the murmur is soft and systolic. After a few weeks it becomes a **continuous "machinery" murmur** under the left clavicle, with **bounding pulses and a wide pulse pressure**.
- **Indomethacin or ibuprofen closes it in PREEMIES ONLY.** It doesn't work in term or older infants.
- Other options: coils or plugs placed in the cath lab (after about 8 months of age), or surgical ligation (no heart-lung bypass needed).

> 🧠 **"PDA = Machinery, Wide, Bounding."**

### B. Obstructive defects: blood is blocked, but stays pink at first
**Coarctation of the aorta (narrowed aorta)**
- **High BP in the arms and low BP in the legs.** Arm pulses are bounding; **femoral pulses are weak or delayed**.
- Older child: headaches and leg pain with exercise. **Rib notching** on x-ray.
- Newborn: crashes when the **ductus closes**, so **PGE1** is given. Infant: fast breathing, poor feeding, cool legs.
- Linked to **Turner syndrome**. Most also have a bicuspid aortic valve.
- After repair: check BP for life. Sports are OK if the **arm-leg BP difference is under 20 mm Hg**.

> 🧠 **"Strong upstairs, weak downstairs."**

**Aortic stenosis (narrowed aortic valve)**
- Usually a **bicuspid** valve. Thickens the **left ventricle**. Boys are affected 4 times as often as girls.
- Harsh grade III–IV murmur at the **URSB that radiates to the neck**, a thrill at the suprasternal notch, and a **narrow** pulse pressure.
- Risks: exertional chest pain, fainting, **sudden death**.
- **Sports:** mild = all sports; moderate = low-intensity only (golf, bowling); severe = no competitive sports.
- A mechanical replacement valve means **warfarin**.

> 🧠 **"Aortic = Left, and into the neck."**

**Pulmonic stenosis (narrowed pulmonary valve)**
- Thickens the **right ventricle**. Usually no symptoms, and found on a routine exam.
- Harsh murmur at the **ULSB that spreads to the back and lungs**. The click **gets quieter when the child breathes in**.
- A **critical** case in a newborn causes cyanosis.
- Balloon valvuloplasty if the pressure gradient is **over 50 mm Hg**.
- Linked to **Noonan and Williams** syndromes.

> 🧠 **"Pulmonic = Pushes the Right side."**

## 5. CYANOTIC defects (right-to-left flow or mixing)
**General points:**
- Cyanosis becomes **visible at about 85%** oxygen saturation. It's easier to see with polycythemia, and harder with anemia or fetal hemoglobin.
- **Polycythemia** helps carry oxygen but raises the **risk of stroke**, so keep these children hydrated.
- **Hyperoxia test:** if 100% oxygen pinks the baby up, the problem is the **lungs**. If the baby stays blue, it's the **heart**.

> 🧠 **The 5 T's, plus HLHS and critical PS:** Truncus (1 trunk), Transposition (2 vessels switched), Tricuspid atresia (3), Tetralogy (4 defects), TAPVR (5 letters)

**Tetralogy of Fallot (the most common cyanotic defect)**
- **PROV:** Pulmonary stenosis, RV hypertrophy, Overriding aorta, VSD
- Cyanosis gets **worse over the first months**. "Pink tets" have mild blockage and little cyanosis.
- **Tet spells** at 2–4 months, triggered by **crying, feeding, or pooping**. Treatment: **knee-chest position**, soothe the baby, oxygen, and morphine. Older children **squat**, which does the same thing.
- Harsh murmur at the left mid-to-upper sternal border. **Boot-shaped heart** on x-ray.
- Repaired in infancy. Follow for life for pulmonary valve leak, arrhythmias, and a widening QRS (sudden death risk).
- Linked to **22q11 (DiGeorge)** and Down syndrome.

> 🧠 **"Tet spell? Tuck like a frog."**

**d-Transposition of the great arteries**
- The aorta comes from the right ventricle and the pulmonary artery from the left. Blood runs in **two separate circles**.
- **Blue within the first hour to day.** Often a **big baby** (infant of a diabetic mother). **Loud single S2.** **Egg-shaped heart** on x-ray.
- Treatment: **PGE1**, a **balloon atrial septostomy** to mix the blood, then an **arterial switch in the first days**. Endocarditis prophylaxis for life.
- **Without treatment, 50% die by 1 month and 90% by 1 year.**

> 🧠 **"Open (PGE1), Mix (septostomy), Switch."**

**Tricuspid atresia (single ventricle)**
- **No valve between the right atrium and right ventricle.** Blood has to cross an ASD to the left side. The right ventricle is small.
- **50% also have TGA.**

> 🧠 **"Front door locked? Take the side door."**

**Hypoplastic left heart syndrome (single ventricle)**
- The left heart is tiny. The **body's blood supply depends on the ductus**, so the baby **goes into shock as the ductus closes**.
- Linked to **Turner and Jacobsen** syndromes.

**Staged surgery for single-ventricle defects:**
1. Newborn: a shunt
2. **4–6 months: Glenn** (SVC connected to the pulmonary artery)
3. **2–4 years: Fontan** (IVC connected to the pulmonary artery)
4. Transplant if the stages fail

- Complications: protein-losing enteropathy, arrhythmias, clots and stroke, low exercise tolerance.
- Endocarditis prophylaxis **while the child is still cyanotic**.

> 🧠 **"Superior first, Inferior last"** (S comes before I in the alphabet).

**Truncus arteriosus**
- One single great artery feeds both the body and the lungs. Linked to **DiGeorge**. **Critical pulmonic stenosis** is also on the cyanotic list.
